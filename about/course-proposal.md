# Course proposal

The proposal submitted to the Sambach study board for the E2026 elective. Reproduced here so
students can see what the course set out to do, and so the co-evaluation in session 10 has something
concrete to argue with. The original Word document is in this folder as
[`course-proposal.docx`](course-proposal.docx).

Author: Laura Horn (ISE), August 2026. The text follows the submitted proposal, with obvious
typographical slips corrected and headings added for readability.

## Living in the Anthropocene: Analysis, Critique, Action

### Background for this proposal

There have been discussions of introducing a mandatory interdisciplinary climate literacy course at
RUC for several years, following the examples of other universities such as Barcelona and DTU. As
part of the Grønne Samtaler process 2025, a working group has focused on this again. Several
pathways have been explored. Despite the support of the rectorate and many good ideas, a combination
of factors including institutional inertia and the impending BA reform has made it a difficult
process. The most promising way to work towards climate literacy seems to be to start with an
elective that can then be scaled up later. The course is partially inspired by a course suggested by
Bjørn Thomassen a few semesters ago, which was not offered in the end.

The climate and ecological emergency demands that students, regardless of discipline, acquire the
analytical tools to understand its causes, consequences, and contested responses. There is no
studying, and no labour market, on a dead planet. This course brings together perspectives from all
four institutes at RUC (ISE, IMT, IKH, INM) into a single interdisciplinary elective. It takes the
Anthropocene, as geological claim, political concept, and cultural condition, as its organising
thread, and asks what it means to be climate literate when the crisis is simultaneously material,
political, affective, and epistemological.

### The pilot

This course is designed as a standalone 5 ECTS elective, and it also serves as a pilot for the
broader ambition of developing a mandatory climate literacy module for RUC students. The course
offers content, format, and interdisciplinary coordination across the four institutes, under
coordination at ISE/Sambach. Session 10 explicitly involves students in evaluating the course and
its design, which gives them ownership and offers a platform for pedagogical reflection. Those
evaluations will be used to formulate recommendations for scaling up.

In practice, students can register for the 5 ECTS course as regular. Each course session is also
announced as an open session for other students to join. There is funding to organise coffee, tea,
and snacks for the students as an incentive. The student assistant working with the sustainability
portfolio at central level helps coordinate with the study administration.

For E2026 the course is coordinated by Laura Horn, who also recruited colleagues from the other
departments. Students are involved in the preparation of the course as far as possible.

### Course structure

Ten standard sessions, taught by lecturers from across RUC's four institutes, with the option for a
guest lecture or excursion. The proposal's preliminary structure, with the institute suggested as
anchor, was:

1. **Introduction: What is the Anthropocene?** (ISE) The Anthropocene as geological claim, political
   concept, and cultural condition. Contested framings, for example individual versus structural
   change. Discussion of the interdisciplinary setup.
2. **Earth system science and planetary boundaries** (INM) Tipping points, carbon cycles,
   biodiversity loss, the biological, physical, and material basis of the crisis. Scientific data and
   frameworks for analysis.
3. **Fossil capital, colonialism, and the making of the Anthropocene** (ISE/IMT) The political
   economy of fossil fuel and fossil dependence. Colonial extraction and resource flows.
   Capitalocene, Plantationocene, and counter-narratives to the Anthropocene.
4. **Feminist perspectives: care, reproduction, and the Anthropocene** (ISE/IMT) What counts as
   work? Reproductive labour, care, and the gendered foundations of the economy. Feminist political
   ecology and the politics of social reproduction.
5. **Communicating crisis: narratives, media** (IKH) Media framings, cultural production, the
   aesthetics and politics of climate communication.
6. **The commons game: governing shared resources** (ISE)
7. **The psychology of climate: denial, disavowal, and the knowledge-action gap** (IMT) Climate
   psychology, anxiety, action, and the affective dimensions of knowing: eco-anxiety, disavowal.
8. **Futures, imaginaries** (ISE/IMT) The role of imaginaries, futures, and forecasting in
   contemporary policies and actions.
9. **From knowledge to action: governance, movements, institutions, strategy** Climate governance,
   international institutions, movements, academic activism, institutional transformation.
10. **Learning in the Anthropocene: evaluation and moving forward** What analytical tools has the
    course provided? What is missing? Structured co-evaluation, in which students contribute to
    designing what climate literacy at RUC could become.

The delivered schedule differs from this outline in wording and in the order of some sessions. The
[schedule in the main README](../README.md#sessions) and the
[course poster](../promotion/anthropocene-course-poster-2026.pdf) are authoritative.

The course complements existing courses. The elective Political Economy of Social-Ecological
Transformation approaches socio-ecological transformation primarily through political economy and
ecological economics, so there is no clash.

### Learning objectives

Students who complete the course should be able to:

1. Identify and critically assess how different disciplines frame the climate and ecological crisis,
   including their respective assumptions, blind spots, and contributions.
2. Analyse the historical, colonial, and gendered dimensions of the Anthropocene as both a material
   condition and a contested concept.
3. Apply interdisciplinary perspectives to concrete problems of collective action and resource
   governance.
4. Critically reflect on the relationship between knowledge production and political action in the
   context of the climate crisis.
5. Communicate analytical arguments that draw on and connect insights from at least two disciplinary
   traditions.
